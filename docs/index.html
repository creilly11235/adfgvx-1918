Reading German radio messages from the last week of the First World War

Where the log stands as of October 17, 2026. 562 of its 688 entries read, 15 of them first published here, and 14 entries on Schmeh’s list still unread.

In the last months of the First World War the German army enciphered its radio traffic between Berlin and its outposts in the Balkans, the Black Sea and the Middle East with the ADFGVX cipher. British and American listening posts intercepted hundreds of the messages, and most of them were finally read by computer in 2017. A few dozen stayed unread, and Klaus Schmeh lists them as #46 on his Top 50 unsolved encrypted messages. This article is about two of them, one with four groups lost in transmission and one under a key nobody had recovered, and about an account of every entry in the log.

How ADFGVX works

The cipher is named after the six letters it transmits, and it works in two steps, a substitution and then a transposition. Here is the German word for attack, ANGRIFF, going through the square the army used from 10 to 12 November 1918 and a four column key.

Each letter or digit is looked up in a 6 by 6 square and replaced by its row label and its column label. A sits in row A, column D, so it becomes AD.

All seven letters go the same way, so ANGRIFF becomes fourteen symbols. Only the six letters A, D, F, G, V and X ever appear, chosen because they sound different from one another in Morse code.

The symbols are written in rows under a numbered key, here 3 1 4 2. The real keys had 15 to 25 columns and were changed every few days, and each part of a long message was transposed on its own.

Each column is read off from top to bottom, in the order of the numbers. Column 1 gives DDDF, column 2 gives XFF, then AXDD and DAD.

The cryptogram goes out in groups of five. To read it the receiver needs both halves of the key, the order of the columns and the 36 cells of the square. Neither half on its own produces anything readable, which is what made the cipher hard in 1918 and still makes short messages hard now.

Childs’s log

James Rives Childs, the American officer who worked on the traffic, kept the intercept logs. They were published in 2000 as an appendix to his book, and in 2017 George Lasry, Ingo Niebel, Nils Kopal and Arno Wacker recovered the keys by computer and read 618 of the 668 cryptograms. The log is split into 688 entries here, one for each row Childs wrote down, and every entry has its own page in the repository.

Each cell is one entry of the log, in the order it was written down, from 19 September 1918 to the weeks after the Armistice. Most are from November, when the traffic was heaviest.

562 entries read under a published key, almost all of them one of the fourteen period keys from the 2017 paper. Some needed a small repair to their length first, where the log had dropped or doubled a symbol.

Klaus Schmeh’s list of unsolved messages has 22 entries, matched here to 22 entries of the log by their opening groups. A few have since been read in the comments on his post, and 14 have no solution published anywhere we could find.

15 entries have a reading published here for the first time. Two of them, the subject of this article, needed a new key or a repaired gap. The rest were waiting in the log under keys already known.

13 read under one of the published keys once the page had been transcribed again. Two readers worked from the scan without seeing each other’s work, and only the symbols they agreed on went forward.

Each then passed the same checks. Encrypted again, it gives back every agreed symbol. Under its key it scores clear of the other keys and of a thousand shuffled copies of itself, and two readers working apart called it German. Where letters are still in doubt, the row says how many stay open.

The table at the end of this article gives the status of every entry on Schmeh’s list, and the full table in the repository gives it for all 688. Leads that have not passed the project’s own checks are not published, so an unsolved row says only that no solution is published here.

Message 2305, four lost groups

On printed page 176 the log has a three part message from LP to ZON, handed in at 23.05 on 10 November and intercepted at 03.38 the next morning, the morning of the Armistice. Parts 2 and 3 read under the key for 10 to 12 November. Part 1 had stayed unread, and the reason is in the log itself.

The header of part 1 promises 240 symbols. The intercept has 220, in 44 groups of five, and nothing marks where the four missing groups fell. A gap like that shifts every later column of the block, so the 220 symbols decrypt to nothing readable.

We tried every position for the 20 missing symbols, one block or four separate groups, 194,756 placements in all, and scored each decryption as German. One placement stands clear, a single block at the start of the fifth line, where the operator skipped half a line. The next best is about 370 times less likely.

With the gap in place the damage turns out to be regular. The lost symbols are the first symbol of every row of the block and the second symbol of rows 10 to 14, so 20 letters each lost one of their two symbols.

The surviving symbol still names a row or a column of the square, so each damaged letter has six candidates rather than 36. The first letter of the message kept its row symbol, A, so it is one of 4, A, R, U, T and 1.

Twenty letters with six choices each make about 3.7 million billion combinations. A dynamic program finds the best scoring one exactly, and the score is plain counting, how often each run of five letters occurs in the 544 messages already read and in ordinary German books.

The result reads as German telegram text from start to finish, the position report of the German 11th Army on the last night of the war as it retreated north out of Serbia across the Danube. X is the operators’ full stop.

Péterréve and Verbász are in the Bácska, Nagybecskerek and Versecz in the Banat, and part 2 carries on with Fehértemplom and the French following. None of that was given to the search. Several of the place names appear nowhere else in the collection, so the score cannot simply have copied them.

Page 153, a new key

Printed page 153, under the heading 7 November, has two parts of a message from FKR to CGH, call signs that appear nowhere else in the collection. Parts 1 to 4 are missing, and neither surviving part reads under the 14 published keys or the keys in Childs’s own appendix. Lasry’s team had reported 14 cryptograms of the right length that no key opened and suspected most were wrongly encrypted. This pair shows that at least some were under a key nobody had recovered.

Together the two parts give 240 symbols, 120 letters, with 15 symbols lost. That is very short for a key search. Lasry’s method worked on a day’s traffic, often thousands of symbols, and in our tests a plain hill climb over both halves of the key never found a planted key this small.

Our search leans on one structural fact. With an even number of columns, each column of the block holds only row labels or only column labels. German favours some rows and columns of the square, so the two kinds look different and can be sorted apart before anything else about the key is known.

Each row label column sits beside a column label column. We rank the pairings and orders by how repetitive their letter stream is, and a hill climb then finds the square for the best of them. The key is an 18 column order on no published list, and only the 24 cells the two parts use are known.

Part 5 reads cleanly, every letter. Part 6 lost one symbol of 15 letters in its three blank groups. The words around them fix twelve, and three near the end stay open. Two more letters rest on a correction to the log, where an A was written for an X.

What survives is a routine order from the retreat, to pass on an earlier order and report how much ammunition the rear guard’s artillery still has. The closing letters are probably a unit and a signature, and with three letters open we leave them unexpanded.

How we know it is right

A reading that merely looks like German is not enough, so each result was tested against controls built the same way.

The page 153 key scores well clear of the next distinct key and far above the best of ten shuffled copies of the pair. We planted 24 messages of the same shape under random 18 column keys. Every exact recovery scored at least the bar shown, and the real pair clears it too.

The best fill of message 2305 scores like a genuine message, and under twelve wrong keys or with the symbols shuffled it scores far lower. We damaged sixty messages that already read in the same way and ran the same procedure. It placed the gap correctly 54 times, and the real message scores like those.

A second search, written without sight of the first result, recovered the same 18 column order. Run blind on a two part message from 13 November, the method returned the key George Lasry published in 2017. Each reading, encrypted again, gives back every surviving symbol, which rules out arithmetic slips though not a wrong reading.

We looked for an earlier solution of both messages and found none. Schmeh lists them as unsolved, the 2017 comments only suggest trying a key search on page 153, the 2018 thesis behind the paper holds none of the phrases, and a web search for them found nothing. We did not check every source, so this is no claim to be first.
