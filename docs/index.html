Reading German radio messages from the last week of the First World War

By Colin Reilly October 13, 2026

Code and data Every message in the log

Where the log stands as of October 13, 2026. 562 of its 688 entries read, 16 of them first published here, and 14 entries on Schmeh’s list still unread.

In the last months of the First World War the German army enciphered its radio traffic between Berlin and its outposts in the Balkans, the Black Sea and the Middle East with the ADFGVX cipher. British and American listening posts intercepted hundreds of the messages, and most of them were finally read by computer in 2017. A few dozen stayed unread, and Klaus Schmeh lists them as #46 on his Top 50 unsolved encrypted messages. This article is about two of them, one with four groups lost in transmission and one under a key nobody had recovered, and about an account of every entry in the log.

How ADFGVX works

The cipher is named after the six letters it transmits, and it works in two steps, a substitution and then a transposition. Here is the German word for attack, ANGRIFF, going through the square the army used from 10 to 12 November 1918 and a four column key.

Each letter or digit is looked up in a 6 by 6 square and replaced by its row label and its column label. A sits in row A, column D, so it becomes AD.

All seven letters go the same way, so ANGRIFF becomes fourteen symbols. Only the six letters A, D, F, G, V and X ever appear, chosen because they sound different from one another in Morse code.

The symbols are written in rows under a numbered key, here 3 1 4 2. The real keys had 15 to 25 columns and were changed every few days, and each part of a long message was transposed on its own.

Each column is read off from top to bottom, in the order of the numbers. Column 1 gives DDDF, column 2 gives XFF, then AXDD and DAD.

The cryptogram goes out in groups of five. To read it the receiver needs both halves of the key, the order of the columns and the 36 cells of the square. Neither half on its own produces anything readable, which is what made the cipher hard in 1918 and still makes short messages hard now.

Childs’s log

James Rives Childs, the American officer who worked on the traffic, kept the intercept logs. They were published in 2000 as an appendix to his book, and in 2017 George Lasry, Ingo Niebel, Nils Kopal and Arno Wacker recovered the keys by computer and read 618 of the 668 cryptograms. The log is split into 688 entries here, one for each row Childs wrote down, and every entry has its own page in the repository.

Each cell is one entry of the log, in the order it was written down, from 19 September 1918 to the weeks after the Armistice. Most are from November, when the traffic was heaviest.

562 entries read under a published key, almost all of them one of the fourteen period keys from the 2017 paper. Some needed a small repair to their length first, where the log had dropped or doubled a symbol.

Klaus Schmeh’s list of unsolved messages has 22 entries, matched here to 22 entries of the log by their opening groups. A few have since been read in the comments on his post, and 14 have no solution published anywhere we could find.

16 entries have a reading published here for the first time. Most read under a published key once the page was read again or a damaged symbol was filled. Two of them, the subject of this article, needed more than that.

The table at the end of this article gives the status of every entry on Schmeh’s list, and the full table in the repository gives it for all 688. Leads that have not passed the project’s own checks are not published, so an unsolved row says only that no solution is published here.

Message 2305, four lost groups

On printed page 176 the log has a three part message from LP to ZON. The operator’s note says it was handed in at 23.05 on 10 November and announces three parts. It was intercepted at 03.38 on 11 November, the morning of the Armistice. Parts 2 and 3 read under the key for 10 to 12 November. Part 1 had stayed unread, and the reason is in the log itself.

The header of part 1 says 240 symbols, and the intercept has only 220, in 44 groups of five. Nothing marks where the missing four groups were. In a transposition cipher a missing stretch shifts every later column of the block, so decrypting the 220 symbols as they stand gives nothing readable.

Every possible position of the 20 missing symbols was tried, one block of 20 or four separate groups of five, 194,756 placements in all, and each decryption was scored as German. One placement stands out, a single block at the start of the fifth line of the log, an ordinary half line skip by the operator. The next best is about 370 times less likely.

With the gap in place the damage is regular. Under this key the lost symbols are the first letter of every row of the block, and the second letter of rows 10 to 14, so 20 letters each lost one of their two symbols.

The surviving symbol still names a row or a column of the square, so each lost letter has six candidates, not 36. The first letter of the message kept its row A symbol, so it is one of 4, A, R, U, T and 1.

Twenty letters with six choices each is about 3.7 million billion combinations. A dynamic program over the message finds the best scoring combination exactly. The score is plain counting, how often each run of five letters occurs in the 544 messages already read and in ordinary German books.

The result reads as German telegram text from start to finish. It is the position report of the German 11th Army on the last night of the war, retreating north out of Serbia across the Danube. X is the operators’ full stop and word separator.

Péterréve and Verbász are in the Bácska, Nagybecskerek and Versecz in the Banat. Part 2 of the same message carries on with Fehértemplom and the French following. None of this context was used in the search. Several of the place names appear nowhere else in the collection, so the score could not simply have copied them.

Page 153, a new key

Printed page 153, under the heading 7 November, has two parts of a message from FKR to CGH, call signs that appear nowhere else in the collection. Parts 1 to 4 are missing. Neither surviving part reads under any of the 14 keys Lasry’s team published, nor under the keys in Childs’s own appendix. Lasry’s team reported 14 cryptograms of the right length that no key opened and suspected most had been wrongly encrypted. This pair shows that at least some were simply under a key nobody had recovered.

Together the two parts give 240 symbols, 120 letters, and 15 of the symbols are lost. That is very short for an ADFGVX key search. Lasry’s method recovered keys from a day’s worth of traffic, often thousands of symbols, and a plain hill climb over both halves of the key never found a planted key of this size in our tests.

The search uses one structural fact. When the key has an even number of columns, every column of the block holds only row labels or only column labels. German telegrams use some rows and columns of the square far more than others, so the two kinds of column have different mixtures of symbols and can be sorted before anything else about the key is known.

Each row label column must sit next to a column label column. The pairings and orders are ranked by how repetitive the resulting letter stream is, and for the best of them a hill climb finds the square. The key is an 18 column order that no published list contains, and only the 24 cells the two parts use can be known.

Part 5 reads cleanly, every letter. Part 6 has 15 letters that each lost one of their two symbols in the three blank groups. Twelve of them are fixed by the words around them and three, near the end, stay open. Two other letters rest on a correction to the log, where an A was written for an X.

What survives is a routine order from the retreat, to pass on an earlier order and report how much ammunition the artillery of the rear guard still has. The closing letters are probably a unit and a signature, and with three letters open we do not try to expand them.

How we know it is right

A reading that merely looks like German is not enough, so each result was tested against controls built the same way.

The page 153 key scores well clear of the next distinct key and far above the best of ten shuffled copies of the pair. Twenty four messages of the same shape were planted under random 18 column keys, and every exact recovery scored at least the bar shown, which the real pair also clears.

The best fill of message 2305 scores like a genuine message. Under twelve wrong keys, or with the symbols shuffled, the best fill scores far lower. Sixty messages that already read were damaged the same way and put through the same procedure, which placed the gap correctly 54 times, and the real message scores like those.

A second search, written without seeing the first result, recovered the same 18 column order. Run blind on a two part message from 13 November, the method returned the key George Lasry published in 2017. And each reading, encrypted again, gives back every surviving intercepted symbol, which rules out arithmetic mistakes though not a wrong reading.

We looked for an earlier solution of both messages and found none. Schmeh lists them as unsolved, the 2017 comments on his post only suggest trying a key search on page 153, the 2018 thesis that expands the 2017 paper holds none of the phrases, and a web search for phrases from the readings found nothing. Not every source was checked, so these are solutions with no earlier solution found, not a claim to be first.

Solved means that every surviving symbol decrypts under the key and the text reads as German. Where a reader filled or left open a few letters, the row says how many. A digit or a signature letter that could take another value does not make a message partial, since there is no word behind it to decide.
